02 · Tokenization

How this works, and how we got here

The tokenizer page shows five recipes and their scores. This one explains what those numbers mean, why the biggest one is the one we rejected, and how forty-five training runs settled the choice. Two of the figures below are draggable; every point in them is a real training run.

A shorthand with a fixed budget

A tokenizer is a shorthand. It gets to invent 10,000 symbols, and all four languages share them. Chunks of text that come up often earn a symbol of their own; everything else has to be spelled out in smaller pieces. If English takes most of the symbols, English is written compactly and Telugu gets spelled out bit by bit.

Fertility is how many pieces it takes to write a given amount of text — lower is better. And the requirements' score is 1000 ÷ (worst fertility − best fertility), which measures only how close the four languages are to each other. It never asks whether the shorthand is any good. That gap between what is measured and what matters is where everything below lives.

The four articles are nothing like the same size

Before any of it makes sense: this is the corpus. One Wikipedia article per language, and English is thirty-two times the size of Maithili. A weight like ×3 means that language's article is fed to the trainer three times over — which is how a 1.8% article gets any say at all.

Hiding a fifth of the corpus does not settle it either

The usual answer to tuning-against-your-own-test is to hold text back. So: hide a fifth of every article, train on the rest, score only the hidden part. Then change which fifth you hide, and watch the ranking rearrange itself.

Fig. 2 — three recipes, five ways of holding text back
Fifteen training runs — three recipes × five slices. The corpus is four articles; a fifth of that is too little text for a score built on the gap between the two extreme languages to settle down.

What we tested, and how

Four things can be varied, and we varied them one at a time, so each row of the results table differs from the benchmark in a single nameable way.

knobwhat it meansvalues tried
Corpus weights How many times each language's article is fed to the trainer. The vocabulary is a fixed 10,000 slots shared by all four, so more copies wins more slots. mai ×2…×16 · te ×5, ×6
Training unit Whether the trainer is handed files — split into lines, so no symbol may cross a line break — or whole documents. Worth ~0.6% of every token count. lines · documents
Algorithm BPE merges character pairs upward; Unigram starts with a large vocabulary and prunes down. Our from-scratch BPE is the same algorithm with no library involved. BPE · Unigram · from‑scratch
Representation Whether symbols are built from bytes or characters. Tested exhaustively in the earlier round, where it turned out to dominate everything else. byte · char

Each result is then checked three ways, in increasing order of how much they can actually settle. One: the benchmark row must reproduce 6502.56 exactly, or the measuring apparatus is wrong and nothing else counts. Two: every row reports its score and its total token count — a row that improves the score while using more tokens has bought fairness by getting worse, which is how the 35,604 recipe was caught. Three: held-out scoring, which failed to settle anything, as Fig. 2 shows.

So two of the three obvious ways to pick a winner do not work. The score can be bought by making the tokenizer worse — the rejected recipe reaches near-perfect evenness by degrading English and Hindi and needs 3,000 more pieces for the same corpus. And the held-out score is too noisy here to rank anything; reported honestly, it mildly favours the recipe we rejected. What is left is what does not move: the score on the whole corpus, and the total number of pieces. On both, the submitted recipe is the best of the honest family — and it is not the biggest number on the page. That one is still there, labelled as rejected, so you can check the choice rather than take our word for it.

Two measurements, kept apart

The tokenizer page has a v1 tab as well. That is an earlier round of experiments on a different corpus — clipped article prose rather than wiki-faithful Markdown — scored in tokens per whitespace word rather than per unit. Its finding stands on its own: representation is the dominant lever, and building symbols out of UTF-8 bytes wrecks Indic scripts. But its scores can never be compared with v2's: the same tokenizer reads about 2.1 under one and 0.6 under the other. That is why they live in separate tabs and are never sorted into one list.
